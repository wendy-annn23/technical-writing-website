Graphs / Charts

Learn how bar graphs, line graphs, and pie charts present information in a clear and visual way.

THE BIG IDEA

Understanding Graphs and Charts

Graphs and charts are visual tools used to present numerical information. They help readers compare values, recognize patterns, understand changes, and interpret data more easily.

In technical writing, they make reports, research findings, and other documents easier to understand.

TYPE 1

1. Bar Graph

Meaning: A bar graph uses rectangular bars to compare quantities or values across different categories. The bars can be vertical or horizontal.

When Is It Used?

  • Comparing the number of students in different year levels.
  • Showing product sales across several months.
  • Comparing attendance across different days.

Remember: Label each category and value clearly. Use a consistent scale to make comparisons accurate.

TYPE 2

2. Line Graph

Meaning: A line graph uses points connected by lines to show how values change over time or across another ordered scale.

When Is It Used?

  • Tracking temperature changes throughout a week.
  • Showing monthly sales trends.
  • Monitoring student enrollment over several years.

Example: Weekly Temperature

This sample line graph illustrates temperatures recorded over five days.

Sample Line Graph

Interpretation: The temperature increased from Monday to Wednesday, decreased on Thursday, and increased again on Friday.

Remember: Line graphs are useful for identifying increases, decreases, and trends. Label both axes and include the units of measurement.

TYPE 3

3. Pie Chart

Meaning: A pie chart is a circular graph divided into slices. Each slice represents a category's proportion of the total.

When Is It Used?

  • Showing how a budget is divided among expenses.
  • Presenting survey responses as percentages.
  • Comparing the proportions of categories in a whole.

Example: Student Preferred Activities

The following illustrative data show the preferred activities of 100 students.

Sample Pie Chart
  • Reading: 40%
  • Sports: 30%
  • Music: 20%
  • Drawing: 10%

Interpretation: Reading is the most preferred activity at 40%, while drawing is the least preferred at 10%.

Remember: All slices together represent 100% of the total. Avoid using too many categories because this can make the chart difficult to read.

4. Comparing the Three Types

Each graph or chart serves a different purpose. Choosing the correct type helps readers understand the information more effectively.

Type Main Purpose Example
Bar Graph Compares different categories. Library visits per day.
Line Graph Shows changes or trends. Temperature throughout a week.
Pie Chart Shows proportions of a whole. Student activity preferences.

5. Important Guidelines

  • Use a clear title. Tell readers what the chart represents.
  • Label the data. Identify categories, axes, and units where applicable.
  • Choose the right chart. Match the chart type to the purpose of your data.
  • Avoid misleading scales. Make visual comparisons fair and accurate.
  • Explain the main finding. Add a short explanation of what the data shows.
  • Identify your data source. Cite the source when using real data in a report.
Remember this ✿

A chart should support your message, not simply decorate the page. Make sure readers can understand what the data measures and where it came from.
